Sesame
Hoagie Roll

A sesame seeded roll built for cheesesteaks. Overnight biga, sturdy open crumb, and a thin crackly crust. The seeded roll that changed how Philadelphia thinks about the cheesesteak.

⚠ Plan ahead — the biga is made the night before

The biga (Italian pre-ferment) needs to sit at room temperature for 12–16 hours before you can mix your dough. After mixing, the rolls proof for 45–60 minutes before baking. Total elapsed time from start to bake: about 15–18 hours. Start the biga the evening before you plan to bake.

⚖ A kitchen scale is essential

All measurements are by weight. Volume cups are not accurate enough for bread dough — especially for small ingredients like yeast where a fraction of a gram changes the result. A basic digital kitchen scale accurate to 0.1g is required before proceeding.

What is a biga?

A biga is a stiff Italian pre-ferment made with flour, water, and a very small amount of yeast. Unlike a poolish (which is wet and pourable), a biga looks rough and shaggy after mixing. That is correct. The long overnight fermentation builds flavor and gluten strength. It is the foundation of traditional South Philly Italian bread.

Must-Have Ingredients
Bread Flour
High protein content (12–13%) is essential for the gluten structure and chewy crumb of a proper hoagie roll. AP flour is supported but produces a softer, less structured result.
King Arthur recommended
Water
Fridge-cold for stand mixer use — friction from the dough hook raises dough temp significantly during mixing. Room temperature for hand mixing.
Filtered or tap
Instant Yeast
Used in both the biga (tiny amount) and the main dough. Active dry yeast is supported but less reliable at the small quantities used in the biga.
SAF recommended
Salt
Do not use iodized table salt — iodine inhibits yeast activity. Fine sea or kosher salt only.
Fine sea or kosher
Neutral Oil or Crisco
Neutral oil (vegetable, canola) produces a slightly moister crumb. Crisco (vegetable shortening) shortens gluten more aggressively and produces a more tender, slightly richer result — closer to old-school American bakery character. Both are flavor-neutral.
Either works
Honey or Sugar
Honey adds subtle moisture retention and a very mild sweetness. Sugar is completely neutral. Both are used in small amounts — flavor difference is minor.
Honey suggested
Sesame Seeds
Applied after an egg white wash before baking. Toasted or raw both work. Be generous: the seeds should coat the entire top and upper sides.
Coat generously
Egg White
Used as the wash to adhere sesame seeds. Egg white only — no yolk. Yolk adds too much color and produces a thicker, matte crust rather than a thin, shiny one.
1 white + 1 tsp water
Semolina or Fine Cornmeal
For dusting the bottom of the pan or baguette tray. Prevents sticking and adds subtle texture to the underside — a traditional Italian bread characteristic.
For pan dusting
Tools
Essential
Kitchen Scale (0.1g accuracy)
Essential for the small amount of biga yeast, which is measured in fractions of a gram.
Instant Read Thermometer
For checking finished dough temperature (FDT) after mixing. Target: 65–70°F. Without it you are guessing.
Bench Scraper
For dividing and handling dough without deflating. Fingers stretch and tear; a scraper cuts cleanly.
Wire Cooling Rack
Rolls must cool on a wire rack — not a cutting board. Airflow underneath keeps the crust firm. A solid surface traps steam and softens the bottom.
Strongly Recommended
Lame or Single-Edge Razor
For scoring the final slash before baking. A serrated knife drags and deflates; a razor cuts cleanly at the low angle needed to open an ear. A sharp paring knife can substitute.
Bread Cloth (Couche) or Substitute
A linen couche supports the sides of the shaped rolls during proofing so they rise upward, not outward. A thick, heavily floured linen towel works as a substitute. A perforated baguette pan does the same structural job.
Stand Mixer with Dough Hook
Produces more consistent gluten development than hand mixing. Requires fridge-cold water due to friction heat during mixing.
Depends on Baking Method
Perforated Baguette Pan
Shapes rolls consistently and circulates heat under the crust. Produces the most uniform result for home bakers without a couche.
Pizza Steel or Stone
For the steel/stone baking method. Provides aggressive bottom heat that mimics hearth baking. Requires a full 45–60 minute preheat at 475°F.
Large Heavy Roasting Pan
For the inverted cover steam method. Must be large enough that it does not touch the rolls. Preheated so it is hot when inverted over the rolls.
Challenger Bread Pan or Cast Iron
Cast iron base plus deep lid traps steam from the dough. Fits 2–3 rolls at a time. Any comparable covered cast iron works.

⚠ The night before — make your biga

The biga must be mixed 12–16 hours before you plan to mix your dough. It sits at room temperature the entire time. To bake in the morning, mix your biga the evening before. To bake in the evening, mix it that morning.

What your biga will look like

Right after mixing it will look rough, shaggy, and dry, not smooth. That is correct for a stiff biga at 60% hydration. After 12–16 hours it should look puffed and domed, feel slightly tacky, and smell lightly yeasty. It will not double the way a wet poolish does.

The Poke Test
With a floured finger, press gently into the side of a shaped roll about half an inch. Under-proofed: springs back immediately and fully. Properly proofed: springs back slowly and mostly, not all the way — bake now. Over-proofed: does not spring back at all — bake immediately.
⚠ Storage — Room Temperature Only

Leave your biga covered at room temperature for the full 12–16 hours. Do not refrigerate - cold temperatures stall fermentation. Cover loosely with plastic wrap or a damp towel to prevent the surface drying out.

Your Biga — Make the Night Before
✓ Biga is ready when

It has expanded noticeably (50–75% larger), looks domed and bubbly on top, smells lightly yeasty and alcoholic, and feels slightly tacky. It will not look smooth — a biga is a stiff dough, not a liquid. A fully collapsed biga is over-fermented — still usable, but reduce your main dough yeast by 25%.

⚠ The meat must be sliced paper thin

Thinly sliced ribeye is what makes a cheesesteak a cheesesteak — not a steak sandwich with melted cheese. Target 1/8 inch or thinner. At this thickness the beef cooks in seconds, stays tender, and molds into the roll.

Slicing the Ribeye
1
Partial freeze method (recommended)

Freeze your ribeye for 45–60 minutes before slicing — firm but not solid. Partially frozen beef slices cleanly and thinly with a sharp chef's knife. Slice across the grain at 1/8 inch or thinner.

⏱ 45–60 min freeze
2
Butcher method (easiest)

Ask your butcher to slice a whole ribeye at 1/8 inch on their commercial slicer. Most counters do this at no charge if you buy the meat there. Most reliable, most consistent.

Why ribeye specifically

Ribeye is the correct cut. Its fat marbling keeps the beef tender and juicy at high heat. Leaner cuts dry out. Sirloin is an acceptable budget substitute if sliced thin enough, but noticeably less juicy.

The Seeded Roll Difference
✓ Lightly toast the interior on the griddle

Unlike a soft roll loaded cold, a seeded roll benefits from a light toast on the cut interior. The toast adds structure so the sturdier roll holds the juicy filling without going soggy, plus a layer of Maillard flavor that complements the sesame crust. Slice three-quarters through with a hinge, place open-face down on the hot surface 30–60 seconds until lightly golden. Do not over-toast — the interior should still yield.
